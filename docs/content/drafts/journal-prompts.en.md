## How to reflect
_Start from what happened, not from what you concluded about yourself._

### rp-001 · What the camera would have caught

- Write one thing that happened today using only what a camera and a microphone could have caught — no adjectives about people.
- why: Once the event and your reading of it sit in different sentences, you have something you can check later.
- follow-up: Which words in your sentence would the camera have missed?

### rp-002 · One feeling, plainly named

- Name today's main feeling in the dullest words you can find, then write the moment it started.
- why: Grand words finish the story too early; a plain name leaves the moment describable.
- follow-up: Would you still use that word if a stranger read the sentence?

### rp-003 · What the feeling made you do

- Pick one action from today and write what it cost you and what it saved you.
- why: Behaviour is easier to verify than mood, and the cost is where the trade-off shows.
- follow-up: Would you pay that cost again tomorrow?

### rp-004 · The thing you skipped

- Write the smallest thing you meant to do today and did not, without explaining why.
- why: Explanation arrives fast and hides the fact; the unexplained gap is the useful part.
- follow-up: What is the first sentence you would write if it got done?

### rp-005 · Less bad than expected

- Find one moment today that turned out less bad than you had predicted, and write what you had expected.
- why: Predictions you never write down can't be corrected; this one can.
- follow-up: How did you know it would be that bad — what was the evidence?

### rp-006 · Where your attention actually was

- Choose one conversation from today and write what you were paying attention to while it happened.
- why: What you listened for shapes what you remember, and the record keeps only one version.
- follow-up: What did the other person probably want you to notice?

### rp-007 · The need you didn't say out loud

- Write one thing you wanted today and asked for differently — or not at all.
- why: Indirect requests are the clearest evidence of what you expect to happen when you ask.
- follow-up: What did you think would happen if you had said it directly?

### rp-008 · The body arrived first

- Write where you felt today's tension physically, and what had happened in the ten minutes before.
- why: A physical marker plus a preceding event gives you two things to compare next time.
- follow-up: Has this pairing shown up in your earlier check-ins?

## Understanding yourself over time
_Look across your own records instead of answering in the moment._

### rp-009 · A line you keep drawing

- Look back through your records and copy out a sentence you have basically written more than once.
- why: Repetition is the only long-term signal this app can honestly show you; the wording is the data.
- follow-up: In the version you wrote first, what did you want that you don't mention now?

### rp-010 · Same situation, different reaction

- Find two entries about a similar situation and write how your reaction differed between them.
- why: Difference inside the same kind of event is the evidence that something moved.
- follow-up: What was different around you, not inside you?

### rp-011 · What you keep proving wrong

- Write a claim about yourself that your own records contradict, and name the record that contradicts it.
- why: A belief loses force when it has a dated counter-example attached to it.
- follow-up: Why does that one entry not count, in your view?

### rp-012 · The condition you keep setting

- Write your most recent "I'll be fine once..." sentence, then list what actually has to happen for it.
- why: Conditions deferred forever are the place where your effort keeps going instead of into an action.
- follow-up: Which part of that condition is inside one of your actions?

### rp-013 · What you did without announcing

- List three things you did in the last month that nobody was told about.
- why: Unannounced actions are the least decorated evidence of what you actually do when it counts.
- follow-up: What do those three have in common that you would not say out loud?

### rp-014 · Reading your older self

- Open an entry from at least three months ago and quote one sentence you would not write today.
- why: Change you can quote is more solid than change you feel; the quote stays checkable.
- follow-up: What did that version of you get right that you still haven't acted on?

### rp-015 · The good weeks looked like something

- Pick your two best weeks in the records and write what was in them that this week lacks.
- why: Concrete conditions from your own history are worth more than generic advice about routine.
- follow-up: Which of those was ever actually under your control?

### rp-016 · The pattern you'd rather not name

- Describe, in neutral words, something that keeps recurring in your entries and that you never comment on.
- why: What a record consistently omits is as informative as what it repeats.
- follow-up: What would change if you wrote it down once, factually?

## Action and outcome
_One action small enough to try, and what you would count as its result._

### rp-017 · Small enough for tonight

- Choose one action you can finish before tomorrow morning and write how you'll know it happened.
- why: An action with a checkable trace becomes a record; without one it stays a wish.
- follow-up: What is the smallest version of that action that still counts?

### rp-018 · What you planned here and actually did

- Pick one action you wrote here earlier and report what happened, in one or two sentences, without grading yourself.
- why: Closing the loop on your own predictions is how this app gets a result instead of a to-do list.
- follow-up: Was the gap in the action, the plan, or the estimate of difficulty?

### rp-019 · If it goes well, what differs

- Before acting, write one observable difference that will exist if this goes well.
- why: A difference you can observe is also a difference you can be wrong about, which is the useful kind.
- follow-up: Who else would notice that difference?

### rp-020 · What has to be true

- Write the two assumptions your next step depends on, and mark which one you have evidence for.
- why: Most stalled steps are waiting on an assumption nobody checked.
- follow-up: What is the cheapest way to test the unchecked one?

### rp-021 · What it cost you

- Write what your last commitment actually cost — time, money, sleep, or something you gave up.
- why: Costs you never write down make every future plan look free.
- follow-up: Would you budget that cost again, knowing the number?

### rp-022 · Where you stopped early

- Describe the moment you quit something that was still working, and what came just before it.
- why: Early stops usually have a trigger, and a trigger is something you can see coming next time.
- follow-up: What would you have needed, in that exact moment, to continue one more step?

### rp-023 · Set the revisit

- Write a date, and the one thing you will look for on that date to decide whether this mattered.
- why: Judgement needs a date attached; otherwise the same question gets re-asked every week without new evidence.
- follow-up: What result would make you call this closed and move on?

## Checking the AI's insights
_An insight earns trust by pointing back at your own records._

### rp-024 · Which record is that claim from?

- Take one AI insight you were shown, and write which of your entries it could have come from. If none, say none.
- why: An insight with no source in your own records is decoration, however accurate it sounds.
- follow-up: Does the insight survive if you remove that entry?

### rp-025 · One counter-example

- For a pattern you were told about, write one entry where it did not hold.
- why: Patterns that survive your own counter-examples are worth acting on; the rest are summaries.
- follow-up: Does the counter-example weaken the pattern or narrow it?

### rp-026 · Read it as a stranger

- Rewrite one insight as if a stranger who had never seen your records wrote it, and note what changes.
- why: Generic wording is the tell that the insight wasn't really about you.
- follow-up: Which half of the rewritten version is still true?

### rp-027 · What would make you reject it

- Write what evidence would make you discard this insight, before deciding whether to keep it.
- why: Deciding the rejection criteria first keeps agreement from being just flattery.
- follow-up: If nothing would make you reject it, what is this sentence actually doing for you?

### rp-028 · Agree, disagree, or partly

- Answer one insight with "agree", "disagree", or "partly" and write the sentence that earns your answer.
- why: The record of your disagreement is what makes the matching insights mean something later.
- follow-up: Which word — agree, disagree, partly — leads to a next action here?

### rp-029 · True but useless

- Find one insight you believe and that you have never acted on; write why acting on it would be hard.
- why: Agreement is cheap. The obstacle after agreement is where your actual constraint lives.
- follow-up: What is the smallest action that would test it despite the obstacle?

### rp-030 · Turn the label back into an event

- Take one word you were described with (avoidant, sensitive, driven...) and write the specific event that earned it.
- why: A label without an event can't be argued with, updated, or deleted — and it shouldn't be trusted.
- follow-up: If that event had gone differently, would the label still apply?
