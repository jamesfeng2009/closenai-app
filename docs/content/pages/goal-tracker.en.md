# A Goal Tracker That Asks How It Actually Went

A normal **goal tracker** records whether you showed up. Streaks, checkboxes, a ring that closes.
That is a real answer to a real question — did I do it — and it is the wrong answer to the one
that would have helped: did doing it produce anything?

We built the second one. The difference shows up in a single column that most goal tracking apps
refuse to have, because it is usually empty and an empty column looks like a product failure
instead of like your week.

## The column nobody else keeps: expected

When you add a goal here, the first field is not the date or the icon. It is a sentence in the
future tense about what you think will happen: *two writing blocks before 9pm, Mon and Wed, and
the draft will be at 1,500 words by Friday.*

That sentence has one job — to be wrong in a specific way.

"Be more consistent" cannot be wrong, so it cannot teach you anything. A prediction can fail on
Thursday and tell you which assumption broke: the time, the length, the meeting that runs late, or
the plan itself.

| What a streak stores | What a prediction stores |
|---|---|
| Did you do it | What you thought would follow from doing it |
| How many days in a row | Whether the number you guessed was the right one |
| A feeling about falling short | A dated difference you can act on |
| Nothing, when you stop | Your last answer, still readable |

## The follow-up mechanic, described plainly

The follow-up is not a notification. There is no "you've got this!" message and no streak-rescue
ping, because those are engagement features wearing the costume of a goal feature.

What happens: a goal carries a date. When the date passes and you have not reported, the entry
stays in your list marked unresolved and the app asks you, next time you open it, one question —
what actually happened. You answer in a sentence. Now the entry has two dated lines and the gap
between them is yours to read.

If you never answer, it stays unresolved. That is the honest state, and it is more useful than a
green tick — a goal journal that marks unfinished things as done is not tracking your goals, it is
managing your feelings about them.

## The template

**One goal per week, written as a prediction you can be wrong about.**

Four lines, once a week, five minutes:

1. **The prediction** — one sentence in the future tense, with a number or a named event in it.
2. **The one action** — the single thing you will do that the prediction depends on. Not three.
3. **The date it gets checked** — a real calendar date, inside 14 days.
4. **The answer, later** — what happened, in facts, plus one change to test.

The rule that makes this work is the 14-day cap. A quarter is long enough for a plan to be
confounded by everything at once, which is another word for unlearnable. Two weeks is short enough
that you usually remember what you meant.

The page on [self improvement you can audit](/en/self-improvement/) covers why the expectation
column exists at all and what to write in the gap box; this page is only about the tracking
mechanic, and does not restate that template.

## Where mood data belongs in this

A **goal tracker** that also charts your feelings will happily invent a correlation. We keep the
two separate on purpose: check-ins give you a 7/30-day trend, goals give you a dated prediction,
and you are the one allowed to connect them. The [mood tracker](/en/mood-tracker/) page explains
what those two windows are for, and what they are not.

## Boundaries

This is record-keeping software. It cannot tell you whether your goal was worth having, and it is
not medical advice — nothing here assesses you, and an unresolved goal row is not evidence about
your character. We also do not promise outcomes: a tool that keeps your predictions honest will
sometimes show you that a goal was the wrong one, which is a useful result but not the one people
hope for.

If a goal list is turning into a reason to punish yourself, or your entries are moving toward
thoughts of hurting yourself, the correct next step is a person: a crisis line or emergency service
in your country, or someone you trust. Our screens put that path before any product suggestion.
