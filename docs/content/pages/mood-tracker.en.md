# Mood Tracker: Check-Ins, Trends and What Came After

A **mood tracker** answers one question well — "how did the week actually go" — and ruins it the
moment it tries to answer a second one you never asked. Most of this category now sells a
verdict: your mood is up 12%, you are trending anxious, here is your score. That second move is
where the instrument stops being useful, because nobody can check it.

This page describes the first version, and the honest way to read it.

## What a check-in is, and what it is not

One tap, plus optional words. That is the whole input, and the constraint is deliberate: a daily
mood journal you have to fill in properly is a habit you will abandon in nine days. The value is
not in the richness of each entry, it is in having enough of them to compare.

What the check-in is not: an assessment. It is your own report, on your own day, in your own
words. Nothing turns it into a classification.

## The two windows, and why there are only two

Our app offers a 7-day view and a 30-day view. Not a free-range date picker, because a free-range
picker is how a chart gets talked into showing whatever you wanted it to show.

Two fixed windows force two different questions:

- **7 days:** what changed since last week, and did I do something about it?
- **30 days:** was last week an exception or a run?

The heatmap is built straight from check-ins. A day with no check-in stays empty — it is not
filled in, smoothed, or interpolated. An empty cell is information about your habit, not a data
quality problem, and inventing a value for it would corrupt the only thing the chart is for.

## The seven-day read

**The 7-day read: three columns — what changed, what stayed, what you will check next week.**

Fill it in on a Sunday, in under five minutes, in this order:

| Column | Rule | Example of a usable line |
|---|---|---|
| What changed | Facts and dates only, no adjectives about yourself | "Tuesday and Thursday checked in lower than the week before." |
| What stayed | Something that did not move — this is the row most people skip | "Evenings have been flat for three weeks running." |
| What to check next week | One thing you can observe, not one thing you promise | "Does the Monday swim meet change Tuesday's number?" |

The third column is what separates a mood tracking app you learn from from a chart you look at.
It turns the read into something that can be wrong next week — which is the only way a trend
teaches you anything.

## What this page will not tell you

It will not tell you that tracking improves mood. Tracking is measurement; measurement does not
change a number by itself. What it can do is stop a week from being remembered as a mood and
start it being remembered as a few dated events, which is the raw material for the harder
question: did the thing I decided actually happen? That is a different object in the product —
see [the goal tracker](/en/goal-tracker/), where the follow-up is the feature.

For the writing side, [self reflection that ends in evidence](/en/self-reflection/) covers what to
do with a low day once you have one.

## Non-medical, plainly

This is a description of a self-recording tool. It is not medical advice, not a screening result,
and not a clinical assessment of you — a mood tracker cannot tell you what is wrong, and a trend
line in an app is not evidence about your health. If two people have the same seven-day curve,
one may be in a bad fortnight and the other in something that needs a professional; the chart
cannot tell them apart, which is exactly why we do not print a conclusion on it.

If your entries are moving toward thoughts of hurting yourself, stop here and contact a crisis
line or emergency service in your country, or someone you trust. In our own screens, that path
appears before any product suggestion, and nothing in the app grades an entry like that.
